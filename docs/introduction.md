
# Memory-Driven Option Pricing: A Multi-Factor Affine Stochastic Volatility Framework for Option Valuation and Mispricing Detection

## Introduction

### Overview

Financial markets are fundamentally driven by uncertainty. The valuation of derivative securities, risk management systems, portfolio construction methodologies, and modern quantitative trading frameworks all depend upon the ability to model uncertainty in a mathematically consistent manner.

Among all sources of uncertainty present in financial markets, **volatility** occupies a uniquely important position.

Volatility governs the magnitude of price fluctuations, determines risk exposure, influences portfolio allocation decisions, affects derivative valuations, and serves as a primary measure of market uncertainty.

As a result, understanding and modeling volatility has become one of the central objectives of modern quantitative finance.

Despite decades of research, accurately modeling volatility remains one of the most challenging problems in financial mathematics. Empirical financial data consistently exhibits complex statistical behavior that cannot be fully explained by traditional models.

These phenomena include:

* Volatility clustering
* Long-memory effects
* Heavy-tailed return distributions
* Leverage effects
* Implied volatility smiles
* Implied volatility skews
* Regime persistence
* Multi-scale volatility dynamics

Collectively, these stylized facts indicate that volatility is neither constant nor driven by a single source of randomness. Instead, volatility behaves as a dynamic and persistent process influenced by market information operating across multiple time horizons.

The primary objective of this research is to develop a mathematically tractable stochastic volatility framework capable of representing these complex dynamics while remaining suitable for practical option valuation and calibration.

### The Evolution of Option Pricing Theory

The foundations of modern derivative pricing were established through the development of the **Black-Scholes-Merton framework**.

The Black-Scholes model represented a revolutionary breakthrough by providing a closed-form solution for European option prices under a set of simplifying assumptions.

The model assumes that asset prices follow a geometric Brownian motion

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sigma S_t \, dW_t
$$

where

* $S_t$ denotes the asset price,
* $\mu$ denotes the expected return,
* $\sigma$ denotes constant volatility,
* $W_t$ denotes a Brownian motion.

The resulting pricing formula transformed financial economics and remains one of the most influential mathematical models ever developed.

However, subsequent empirical evidence revealed significant discrepancies between theoretical predictions and observed market behavior. Market option prices imply volatility surfaces exhibiting rich structures that cannot arise under constant volatility assumptions.

Observed markets display:

* Volatility smiles
* Volatility skews
* Time-varying variance
* Persistent volatility shocks

These observations motivate the need for more sophisticated volatility models.

### From Constant Volatility to Stochastic Volatility

To address the limitations of constant-volatility frameworks, **stochastic volatility models** were introduced.

The central idea is straightforward: Rather than assuming volatility remains fixed, volatility itself becomes a stochastic process.

A generic stochastic volatility system can be represented as

$$
dS_t = \mu S_t \, dt + \sqrt{v_t} S_t \, dW_t
$$

where $v_t$ represents an evolving variance process.

This innovation significantly improves the ability of models to reproduce observed option-market phenomena.

Among stochastic volatility models, the **Heston framework** emerged as one of the most influential due to its balance between realism and tractability. The Heston model introduces a stochastic variance process

$$
dv_t = \kappa(\theta - v_t) \, dt + \xi \sqrt{v_t} \, dZ_t
$$

which preserves affine structure and admits semi-closed-form option pricing formulas. As a result, Heston-type models became standard tools throughout quantitative finance.

### Remaining Limitations of Classical Stochastic Volatility Models

Although stochastic volatility models represent a substantial improvement over constant-volatility approaches, important limitations remain.

Most classical stochastic volatility models rely upon a **single latent variance factor**. This assumption implicitly suggests that volatility persistence is generated by one underlying mechanism.

Empirical evidence suggests otherwise. Financial markets display volatility persistence across multiple horizons simultaneously. A market shock may generate:

* Immediate effects lasting hours,
* Intermediate effects lasting days,
* Long-term effects lasting months.

These effects interact but decay at different rates. For example:
- A central-bank announcement may create large short-term volatility.
- Macroeconomic uncertainty may influence volatility over several weeks.
- Structural economic changes may affect volatility over months or years.

A single variance factor cannot naturally separate these distinct persistence mechanisms.

Consequently, many classical models struggle to simultaneously reproduce:

* Short-term implied volatility dynamics,
* Long-term volatility persistence,
* Multi-scale clustering behavior,
* Cross-maturity surface structures.

These limitations motivate the introduction of **memory-driven volatility models**.

### Volatility as a Multi-Horizon Memory Process

One of the central observations motivating this framework is that markets possess **memory**. Historical volatility shocks influence future volatility behavior long after the original shock has occurred.

Importantly, this influence is not uniform across time. Different components of the market remember information over different horizons.

The aggregate volatility process therefore reflects the interaction of multiple memory scales. This observation motivates the representation of volatility as a superposition of memory components

$$
U_t^{(1)}, \, U_t^{(2)}, \, \ldots, \, U_t^{(m)}
$$

where each component captures persistence at a particular time horizon.

Rather than treating volatility as a single latent variable, the proposed framework models volatility as a collection of interacting memory states. This decomposition allows volatility persistence to emerge naturally from the interaction of multiple factors.

### Objectives of the Proposed Framework

The proposed model is designed to satisfy several objectives simultaneously.

**Objective 1:** Capture Volatility Clustering — Periods of elevated volatility should naturally lead to future periods of elevated volatility.

**Objective 2:** Incorporate Multiple Memory Horizons — Different persistence mechanisms should be represented independently.

**Objective 3:** Preserve Mathematical Tractability — The model should remain analytically manageable despite increased complexity.

**Objective 4:** Maintain Affine Structure — Affine models possess powerful mathematical properties that facilitate pricing and calibration.

**Objective 5:** Admit Characteristic Function Solutions — The future distribution of asset prices should be computable through characteristic-function methods.

**Objective 6:** Enable Efficient Option Pricing — The framework should support Fourier-transform valuation techniques and FFT acceleration.

**Objective 7:** Support Calibration to Market Data — Model parameters should be estimable using observed option prices.

**Objective 8:** Generate Systematic Mispricing Signals — The framework should provide a direct connection between volatility forecasts and trading opportunities.

### Central Research Philosophy

The philosophy underlying this project is that option pricing should not be viewed as an isolated problem. Instead, option valuation forms part of a larger quantitative pipeline.

The complete workflow can be represented as

Market Information
        ↓
Volatility Dynamics
        ↓
Memory-State Estimation
        ↓
Fair Option Valuation
        ↓
Market Comparison
        ↓
Mispricing Detection


Under this perspective, option pricing becomes a mechanism for converting information about volatility into economically meaningful trading signals.

The model itself does not attempt to predict market direction. Instead, it seeks to estimate fair values conditional upon the current volatility environment. Any discrepancy between theoretical and observed prices may then be interpreted as potential market mispricing.

### Contribution of This Research

The primary contribution of this work is the construction of a **multi-factor memory-based stochastic volatility framework** that combines:

* Multi-horizon volatility persistence,
* Affine process theory,
* Characteristic-function methods,
* Fourier-based valuation,
* Efficient calibration procedures,
* Mispricing detection capabilities.

The framework establishes a direct mathematical bridge between volatility forecasting and derivative valuation.

By preserving affine tractability while incorporating multiple interacting memory factors, the model seeks to extend the flexibility of classical stochastic volatility models without sacrificing computational feasibility.

The resulting system forms the foundation for the theoretical developments presented throughout this repository. Subsequent chapters develop the mathematical structure of the model, derive its characteristic-function representation, construct the associated Riccati system, establish pricing methodologies, analyze calibration procedures, and investigate applications to systematic option mispricing detection.
